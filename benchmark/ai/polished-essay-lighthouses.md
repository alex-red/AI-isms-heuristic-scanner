When Lighthouses Lost Their Keepers

Most American lighthouses were automated in the second half of the twentieth century. Before that, a keeper lived on every rock and headland that mattered, and each one had a log, a lamp to trim and a set of lenses to polish with rouge and soft cloth.

Automation removed the labor but also a kind of record. Keepers wrote down weather, passing ships, wrecks and the hours they spent awake. A century of those logs is now one of the better sources for coastal climate history, because the entries were made at fixed times, by people who had no reason to exaggerate. A sensor logs a wind speed, but it does not note that the fog came in at four and the foghorn ran until noon.

There is a cost argument for automation, and it is a good one. An unmanned light with an LED array draws a few hundred watts and needs service twice a year. A staffed station needed housing, supply boats and families. Many stations were also dangerous. Keepers drowned going down to the landing, and the lenses were a fire risk.

The loss is narrower than the nostalgia suggests. Nobody needs the keepers back to keep ships off the rocks. What we lost is a habit of continuous, human observation at a hundred fixed points on the coast, and nothing has replaced it.
